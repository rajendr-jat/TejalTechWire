Why Vijay Pande is betting small on AI medicine after running billions at a16z

Vijay Pande, a well-known scientist and investor, recently made a big career pivot. For over 10 years, he ran a massive healthcare and life sciences fund at the tech venture capital firm a16z, managing close to $4 billion. Now, he has walked away from that giant fund to start a much smaller firm called VZVC, where he plans on betting small by making only a few highly focused investments each year.

Pande believes that the future of medicine lies in artificial intelligence (AI). He wants to use his new, smaller fund to back companies that are using AI to design better drugs and make healthcare more personalized. Instead of guessing which treatments might work for a broad group of people, he thinks computers can help us create "precision medicine," which means treatments tailored specifically to your own body.

How AI is changing drug design

Traditionally, finding new medicines has been a slow and lucky process. Drug companies often test treatments on animals like mice, but these tests only predict how a drug will work in humans about 20% of the time. This high failure rate is why clinical trials—the human testing phase of drug development—cost hundreds of millions of dollars. Pande explains that AI can analyze complex biological data much better than animal testing can, which could make clinical trials faster, cheaper, and far more successful.

The rise of precision medicine

Another major goal for Pande's new venture is advancing precision medicine. This term simply means medical care that is customized for each individual patient rather than using a one-size-fits-all approach. For example, when you get a blood test today, doctors compare your results to the general public average. With AI, doctors will soon be able to compare your current health data against your own personal history to spot exactly what is wrong and give you the right drug on the very first try.

The challenge with medical data

While AI has incredible potential in healthcare, it faces a unique hurdle. In other industries, AI programs can learn by reading massive amounts of free text and images on the internet. However, medical and biological data is highly private and cannot just be scraped off the web. This means that every medical AI startup must build its own private, secure database, making the race to build these medical tools highly competitive.

Quick Questions Answered

Why did Vijay Pande leave his role managing $4 billion?

He wanted to focus on a smaller, more selective investment strategy that makes only a few concentrated bets on AI-driven medicine each year.

What is precision medicine?

It is a personalized approach to healthcare where treatments and drug prescriptions are tailored to your specific body and medical history rather than a general average.

Why do so many new drugs fail before reaching patients?

Most drugs are designed using animal models like mice, which do not accurately predict how the human body will react, causing 80% of drugs to fail during human clinical trials.

Sony and Warner Chappell are suing Anthropic over music copyright

The music giants Sony and Warner Chappell are suing the artificial intelligence company Anthropic. They claim the AI company committed a blatant copyright violation by taking thousands of songs. The music publishers are calling this lawsuit a massive case of property theft.

This lawsuit matters because artificial intelligence (AI) systems need huge amounts of data to learn how to write text or songs. To get this data, companies like Anthropic often copy files from the internet. Music companies argue that doing this without permission is illegal, and they want the AI company to pay billions of dollars in damages.

What Anthropic is Accused of Doing

The music publishers say Anthropic ran a massive campaign of downloading, scraping, and torrenting copyrighted works. Scraping means using automated software to pull information off websites, while torrenting is a way of downloading files from other users online. The lawsuit alleges that Anthropic employees used piracy websites to download over 5 million books. It also claims they scraped lyrics from websites that pay licensing fees to use the songs. Famous songs like "September" by Earth, Wind & Fire and "Hallelujah" by Leonard Cohen were allegedly copied to train Anthropic's Claude AI assistant.

The Costs and Past Legal Battles

The music companies are asking the court for up to $150,000 for each stolen song. They also want up to $25,000 every time copyright information was stripped from a file. If the music publishers win the maximum amount, the total bill could reach several billion dollars. Anthropic has faced legal trouble over copyright in the past. The company recently settled a different lawsuit with book publishers for $1.5 billion, and other music companies are also taking them to court.

Who Is Being Sued

The lawsuit does not just target the AI company itself. It also names Anthropic's 2 co-founders, Dario Amodei and Benjamin Mann, as personal defendants. The legal complaint claims that Mann personally used software to download over 5 million pirated files to build the Claude AI models.

Quick Questions Answered

Which songs are named in the lawsuit?

A: The lawsuit mentions famous songs, including "September" by Earth, Wind & Fire, "Livin' on a Prayer" by Bon Jovi, and "Paper Rings" by Taylor Swift.

How much money are the music publishers asking for?

A: They are asking for up to $150,000 for each copyrighted work that was copied, plus up to $25,000 for each instance of removed copyright data.

Has Anthropic been sued before?

A: Yes, Anthropic was sued by book authors and ended up settling that case for $1.5 billion, and it faces other active lawsuits from music groups like Universal Music Group.

Sony and Warner Sue Anthropic Alleging Brazen Music Theft

Some of the world's biggest music companies, including Sony Music and Warner Music, are taking the artificial intelligence giant Anthropic to court. The music publishers are alleging a brazen campaign of intellectual property theft. Intellectual property is the legal term for creative work that belongs to someone, such as songs, art, or books. The companies claim the tech firm illegally downloaded copyrighted songs to train its popular AI assistant, Claude.

This massive lawsuit was filed in a California federal court. The music companies claim Anthropic used piracy and illegal torrent sites to download millions of pages of copyrighted books, sheet music, and song lyrics. Torrent sites are online networks where people share files, often illegally, without paying for them.

What the Music Labels Claim

The music publishers are very angry because they say Anthropic did not ask for permission or pay to use their music. Instead, the publishers claim Anthropic took a massive shortcut by downloading huge amounts of copyrighted material from the internet. This includes lyrics and sheet music from famous artists. The music companies call this flagrant piracy and want the court to make the AI company stop using their work.

Anthropic's History of Legal Trouble

This is not the first time Anthropic has faced trouble over copyright issues. In fact, a judge recently ordered the AI company to pay $1.5 billion in a similar case brought by a group of authors. In that landmark case, the court ruled that while using copyrighted material to train AI is sometimes allowed, getting that material through illegal piracy is completely against the law. Anthropic is also facing active lawsuits from other major music groups.

Quick Questions Answered

Who is suing Anthropic in this new case?

A: Sony Music Publishing, Warner Chappell, and several other music publishers have filed the lawsuit together.

What exactly is Anthropic accused of doing?

A: The music publishers claim Anthropic used illegal torrenting and piracy websites to download millions of copyrighted works, including song lyrics and sheet music, to train its AI model named Claude.

Has Anthropic lost any similar lawsuits before?

A: Yes, Anthropic was recently ordered to pay $1.5 billion in a lawsuit after a judge ruled the company illegally used pirated books to train its AI.
